📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What changes in a noun when it shifts from subject to direct object?
You already know that a 1st declension noun ending in -a is the subject. In Puella nauta est ("The girl is a sailor"), both puella and nauta are nominative — they're the same person, linked by est. This module, that pattern is about to get more powerful. This whole new idea starts with one letter.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what a direct object is, in my own words.
2
I will be able to notice when a familiar noun's ending has changed from -a to -am.
3
I will be able to spot little words like ad and per showing up next to that new ending.

📚 PART 1 · WORDS WE KNOW

Before any new ending, warm up with words you already half-know. Each one left a fingerprint on English.

aqua — you already know it as a color (blue-green, like water)
terra — you know "terrain" and "territory"
via — English uses it directly: "I traveled to Chicago via Detroit"
insula — you know "insular" and "peninsula"
patria — you know "patriot"
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
The Latin word insula means island. Which English word do you think grew from it?
📚 PART 2 · NOTICE SOMETHING?

Read these three Latin phrases — don't worry about naming anything yet

Romans described travel constantly. Here are three little travel phrases built from words you already know:

ad insulam
toward the island
per silvam
through the forest
trans aquam
across the water

Compare each word to the one you already know: insula → insulam. silva → silvam. aqua → aquam. Every one of them picked up the same new ending after a little word like ad, per, or trans.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
What do you notice happens to the end of the noun right after words like ad, per, and trans?
💡 Heads-up for next lesson: that new -am ending has a name, and it does much more than follow little words like ad and per. Tomorrow you'll meet it in full.
