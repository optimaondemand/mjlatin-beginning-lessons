📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Which five words always demand the accusative?
Back in Lesson 4.01 you noticed little words like ad and per sitting next to an -am ending. Today they get names: the five accusative-only prepositions. There is no exception — whenever you see one, the noun that follows is accusative.

📚 PART 1 · ONE ENDING, TWO JOBS

You now know two ways a 1st declension noun appears in the accusative:

Direct object
receives the action of amat: Puella nautam amat.
Object of a preposition
follows ad, per, trans, ante, or post: ad insulam

Different jobs, same ending: -am. The accusative is the case of the endpoint — the target, the thing acted upon or moved toward.

⚠️ Watch out: if you ever see one of these five prepositions followed by a noun ending in -a (nominative), that's an error. Trust the rule: preposition + accusative, every time.
📚 PART 2 · ONE PATTERN, MANY WORD ORDERS

Because Latin endings — not word position — carry the grammar, a Latin sentence can be rearranged without changing its meaning. All three of these mean the same thing: "The girl loves the woman."

Puella feminam amat.
Feminam puella amat.
Feminam amat puella.

In English, "The woman loves the girl" means something completely different from "The girl loves the woman" — word order carries the grammar. In Latin, the -a on puella and the -am on feminam do that work instead, so poets like Vergil could arrange words for rhythm and drama without ever confusing their readers.

The reader's checklist: find the preposition, find the noun after it, confirm it ends in -am, then translate preposition + noun together.
