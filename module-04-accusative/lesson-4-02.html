Can one letter turn a subject into a direct object?

📚 PART 1 · THE ACCUSATIVE CASE

The direct object ending is -am

Latin distinguishes not only subjects from predicates but subjects from direct objects — the noun that receives the action of a verb. It does this with a single change: the accusative ending -am on 1st declension nouns.

Puella nauta est.
The girl is a sailor. — both nominative
Puella nautam amat.
The girl loves the sailor. — subject + direct object

One letter — the m — transforms nauta the predicate nominative into nautam the direct object, and the verb changes from a linking verb (est) to a transitive verb (amat) — a verb whose action crosses over to an object. Word order stays the same; the ending does all the work.

💡 amat means "loves" or "likes." You don't need to conjugate it yet — just know it as a vocabulary item that always takes a direct object.
📚 PART 2 · THE DIRECT OBJECT TEST

When you read a sentence with amat, ask two questions:

1
Who is doing the action? That noun is nominative (-a). It's the subject.
2
Who or what receives the action? That noun is accusative (-am). It's the direct object.

In Femina puellam amat, the woman (-a) loves; the girl (-am) is loved. You don't need to know where each noun falls in the sentence — the endings tell you everything.

📚 PART 3 · SUBJECT OR OBJECT?

Read the ending and decide: subject (nominative) or direct object (accusative)?
