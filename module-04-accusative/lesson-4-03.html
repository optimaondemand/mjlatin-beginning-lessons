📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why did the Romans need a case just for movement and travel?
A civilization that built more than 50,000 miles of roads talked about travel constantly. Today's vocabulary — roads, water, land, home — is the vocabulary Roman writers used to describe a world on the move.

📚 PART 2 · THE ROMAN ROAD NETWORK

A world built for movement

The Via Appia (Appian Way), begun in 312 BC, ran from Rome south to Brundisium — the port of departure for Greece and the East. Its name means "Appian Road": via is one of today's vocabulary words. Roman roads were not just practical engineering — they were instruments of empire, carrying soldiers, governors, and merchants across every province.

When Julius Caesar wrote his Commentarii de Bello Gallico (Commentaries on the Gallic War) — his first-person military dispatches from his conquest of Gaul in the 50s BC — his prose is famous for its clarity and its relentless geography. His armies march through forests, advance toward rivers, cross boundaries. Every directional phrase uses the accusative case you are learning this module.

💡 Why it matters: Latin uses the accusative as the case of destination and direction. Learning it is, in part, learning to think like a Roman who moves across the world.
📚 PART 3 · DERIVATIVE MINUTE

Each of today's words left a mark on English:

via → viaduct (via + ductus, "led" — a structure that leads a road over an obstacle)
aqua → aquatic, aqueduct
terra → terrain, territory
patria → patriot, patriotism
insula → insulate, peninsula
