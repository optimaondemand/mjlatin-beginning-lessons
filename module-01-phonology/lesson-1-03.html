📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why isn't Caesar pronounced “SEE-zar”?
English trained your ear to expect certain letters to change sound depending on what follows them. Latin doesn't play by those rules. Today you'll unlearn four English habits — for C, G, V, and J.

📚 PART 2 · FOUR LETTERS, FOUR SURPRISES

Every one of these letters looks familiar — but each behaves in a way English never taught you to expect.

LetterRuleExample
CAlways /k/ — never soft like cityCaesar = KAI-sar
GAlways hard — never like gemGallia = GAL-lee-a
VPronounced /w/ — never like English vvīnum = WEE-num
J / IPronounced /y/ — never like English jiam = YAHM
💡 Double consonants are held, not softened: Gallia has a real double l — say GAL—lee—a with a small hold on the l, the way Italian still does today.

Julius Caesar's own famous line — veni, vidi, vici (“I came, I saw, I conquered”) — sounds like WAY-nee, WEE-dee, WEE-kee in classical pronunciation, not “VEE-nee, VEE-dee, VEE-chee.” That churchier-sounding version is a different, later tradition (Ecclesiastical Latin) — this course teaches the classical Roman sound.

📚 PART 3 · DERIVATIVE MINUTE

These English words descend straight from today's sound rules:

vōx (voice, said WOKS) → vocabulary
iuvenis (said YOO-wen-is) → juvenile
cōnsonāre (to sound together) → consonant
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
English juvenile starts with a hard “j” sound. What does that tell you about how the word's sound changed on its way from Latin iuvenis (said YOO-wen-is) into English?
