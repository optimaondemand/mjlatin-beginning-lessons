📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Can two vowels ever glide together as a single sound?
Yesterday you sorted short vowels from long ones. Today you chant the whole vowel system out loud — and meet the three Latin diphthongs, pairs of vowels that blend into one syllable.
🎵 MEMORY WORK
Chant the five short vowels, then the five long vowels, in order:
a · e · i · o · u  —  ā · ē · ī · ō · ū
Say it forward five times today, then try it backwards: ū, ō, ī, ē, ā — u, o, i, e, a.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to recite the full Latin vowel chant, forward and backward.
2
I will be able to name Latin's three diphthongs and pronounce each one.
3
I will be able to tell a true diphthong apart from two vowels that are simply next to each other.
📚 PART 1 · THREE SOUNDS THAT TRAVEL TOGETHER

A diphthong is two vowels gliding together as one syllable, not two. Latin has three:

ae
like “ai” in aisle — heard in Graecia
au
like “ou” in house — heard in Augustus
oe
like “oy” in boy — found in Greek borrowings
💡 If two vowels sit side by side but are not one of these three pairs, pronounce them as two separate syllables. Italia has ia together — but that's two syllables (li-a), not a diphthong.
📚 PART 2 · WHY IT MATTERS TO GET IT RIGHT

The diphthong ae gave us Graecia (Greece) and appears in Caesaris (of Caesar). The diphthong au lives on in Augustus — the emperor whose name became a month, a title, and a byword for grandeur across European languages. Romans encountered oe mostly in words borrowed from Greek, a language and culture they absorbed constantly.

Getting the diphthong right is part of hearing Latin the way a Roman would have — not sound by sound, but syllable by syllable.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Only three vowel pairs glide as one sound in Latin: ae, au, oe. Every other pair of vowels stays two separate syllables. Say the chant once more before you go: a, e, i, o, u — ā, ē, ī, ō, ū.
