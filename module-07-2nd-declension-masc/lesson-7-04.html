📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What happens to servus when there's more than one — and are all masculine nouns spelled alike?
Today you finish the paradigm with the plural forms, then meet two nouns that break the pattern you just learned. One is a small spelling surprise. The other teaches you something important about how Latin gender really works.
🎵 MEMORY WORK
Your target is the complete four-cell paradigm for servus. Chant it aloud — singular row first, then plural:
SingularPlural
Nominative (subject)servusservī
Accusative (object)servumservōs
Say each cell as case — number — ending — form: “nominative singular, -us, servus… nominative plural, -ī, servī… accusative singular, -um, servum… accusative plural, -ōs, servōs.” Then say it backwards.

📚 PART 1 · ONE BECOMES MANY, AGAIN
servī
the slaves — the subject  👤👤👤
servōs
the slaves — the object  👤👤👤

Add the new vocabulary word for this lesson: populus, populī m. — people, nation. And a proper-noun adjective you'll see constantly: Rōmānus, Rōmānī m. — a Roman man, a Roman citizen.

The 1st declension you already know follows the same idea, with different endings. Compare the two side by side:

1st decl. (puella)2nd decl. masc. (servus)
Nom. pl.puellaeservī
Acc. pl.puellāsservōs
💡 The endings differ, but the job doesn't. Nominative plural always marks the subject, whether it ends in -ae or -ī. Accusative plural always marks the object, whether it ends in -ās or -ōs. Once you know a declension's endings, you can parse any noun in it.
📚 PART 2 · TWO WORDS THAT BREAK THE RULES
⚠️ Surprise #1 — vir (man)
Vir, virī m. is a 2nd-declension masculine noun, but its nominative singular is just vir — there is no -us ending. The stem is already complete on its own. Every other form follows the pattern normally (genitive virī, and so on). You simply memorize this one as an exception.
⚠️ Surprise #2 — agricola and nauta (farmer, sailor)
Agricola, agricolae m. — farmer — and nauta, nautae m. — sailor — look exactly like 1st-declension nouns. They decline exactly like puella: agricola, agricolam, agricolae, agricolās. But their dictionary entry says m., not f. They are grammatically masculine.
Why? Because the people these words name — farmers, sailors — were overwhelmingly male in Roman society, and Latin marks the grammatical gender accordingly, even though the word itself uses 1st-declension endings.

The lesson here is precise: declension and gender are two separate facts. Declension tells you which set of endings a noun uses. Gender is a separate label the dictionary gives you. Don't confuse them — and don't assume a noun's ending always tells you its gender.

📚 PART 3 · THE MIXED-DECLENSION CHECKLIST

Now that you know two declensions, sentences can combine them: Agricola servum amat. (The farmer loves the slave.) Here agricola is 1st declension in form, masculine in gender, nominative singular — the subject. Servum is 2nd declension masculine, accusative singular — the object. Latin holds a sentence together by case, not by matching endings.

Run this checklist on any noun you meet:

1
Read the ending. Which declension's pattern does it match?
2
Decide the case and number the ending signals: subject or object, one or many?
3
If the noun looks like agricola, nauta, or vir, remember: the ending and the gender may not match — check the dictionary label, not just the spelling.
