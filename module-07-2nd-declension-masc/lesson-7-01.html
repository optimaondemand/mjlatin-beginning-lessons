📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Does every noun in Latin work the same way?
You know puella inside and out: puella, puellam, puellae, puellās. But Latin has more than one pattern for nouns. Today you meet a brand-new one — and a new idea that comes with it: every Latin noun has a grammatical gender.

📚 PART 2 · A NEW CATEGORY: GRAMMATICAL GENDER

Every Latin noun belongs to a gender — and it isn't about biology

In Latin, every noun belongs to one of three grammatical genders: masculine, feminine, or neuter. This can be confusing at first, because the word “gender” sounds like it should be about the sex of a person or animal. Sometimes it lines up that way — puella (girl) is feminine, which matches. But grammatical gender is really a label for how a noun behaves, not a fact about biology. You will even meet nouns later that look feminine in their endings but are labeled masculine — because the person the word names is male. The dictionary always tells you the gender; you memorize it along with the word.

💡 Think of it like a filing system. Every noun gets filed under masculine, feminine, or neuter — and that filing affects its endings. You don't get to choose the file; the dictionary tells you which one.

Meet servus

Servus means “slave” or “servant.” It is a noun, just like puella — but look at what happens to its ending:

servus
the slave — the subject  👤
servum
the slave — the object  👤

The subject ending is -us, not -a. The object ending is -um, not -am. You are looking at a brand-new pattern: the 2nd declension masculine. Latin groups its nouns into families called declensions, and this is your second one.
