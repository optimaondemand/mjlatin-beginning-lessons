📚 PART 2 · THE ROMAN HOUSEHOLD

One household, many roles

Roman slavery was not a small or marginal institution. Historians estimate that by the late Republic, roughly one-third of the people living in Italy were enslaved. Enslaved people did not only labor in fields — inside a wealthy household (the familia), they worked as teachers, accountants, doctors, secretaries, and craftsmen. A large Roman household could include dozens, even hundreds, of enslaved persons, all under the authority of the paterfamilias — the father and master of the household.

The word familia itself is instructive: it comes from famulus, meaning household servant. Roman law gave the paterfamilias a striking amount of authority — called patria potestas — over everyone in the household, including his own adult filius. Sons were free citizens and heirs, yet still legally subordinate to their father for as long as he lived. When you say filius dominī — “the son of the master” — you are naming a relationship of both family affection and legal hierarchy at once.

💡 Friendship, Roman-style. An amicus was not always just a private friend — the word often meant something closer to a political ally. When the writer Cicero opened his letters “M. Cicero S.D. Amico Suo” (Marcus Cicero sends greetings to his friend), the word carried social and political weight, not just warmth.

This is what makes Latin more than a grammar exercise: the words themselves are a window into how a whole civilization organized itself.
