Latin in English — You Already Know More Than You Think

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How much of the English you speak every day is secretly Latin?
English isn't a Romance language — it comes from the Germanic dialects the Anglo-Saxons brought to Britain. But English still absorbed an enormous amount of Latin vocabulary. Today you'll see how, and start collecting the Latin roots hiding inside words you already use.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to describe two ways Latin vocabulary entered English historically.
2
I will be able to estimate how much of English vocabulary has Latin roots.
3
I will be able to trace at least four English words back to their Latin root.
📚 PART 1 · TWO ROADS INTO ENGLISH

Latin vocabulary reached English through two main historical channels.

Road 1 — 1066 AD
The Normans conquered England and brought Old French — itself a Romance language — which carried thousands of Latin-derived words into English.
Road 2 — the Renaissance
Scholars deliberately imported Latin terms directly into English to name new scientific, legal, and philosophical ideas.
💡 As a result, roughly 60 percent of English vocabulary has Latin or Latin-via-French roots: animal (from animal, “living thing”), education (from educatio, “a leading out”), science (from scientia, “knowledge”), justice (from justitia, “righteousness”), library (from libraria, “bookshop”). These aren't distant coincidences — they're direct inheritances. You already know more Latin than you think.

📚 PART 3 · DERIVATIVE MINUTE

terra → Mediterranean. Terra means “earth” or “land.” Mediterranean comes from medius (“middle”) + terra (“land”) — “the sea in the middle of the land.” Surrounded by that sea on three sides, the Romans thought of it as the center of the world — which, for them, it was.

via → obvious, trivial. Via means “road” or “way.” Obvious comes from ob (“in the way of”) + via — literally “in the road,” something standing right in front of you. Trivial comes from trivium (“three roads” — a crossroads where common people gathered), and originally meant “of the common roadside.”
