📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How did one language become five languages — and yet also stay exactly the same?
Rome fell in 476 AD, but Latin didn't disappear — it split into two paths. Today you'll trace both, and memorize six Latin abbreviations you already see in English every week.

📚 PART 1 · ONE LANGUAGE, TWO PATHS

When the Western Roman Empire collapsed in 476 AD, Latin did not disappear. It split into two paths.

Everyday spoken Latin
slowly evolved, region by region, into French, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, and Romanian — the Romance languages
Written, scholarly Latin
stayed the same for centuries — a shared language for the Church, universities, and royal courts across borders

Because scholarly Latin held so steady, a monk in Ireland around 800 AD and a scholar in Bologna, Italy, around 1200 AD could both read the very same Latin text without confusion — even though the everyday languages spoken around them were completely different.

💡 Heads-up: “Romance” here has nothing to do with love stories. It comes from Romanus, “Roman.” A Romance language is simply one descended from Roman speech.
🎵 MEMORY WORK · SIX ABBREVIATIONS YOU ALREADY USE
These six Latin abbreviations turn up in English writing constantly. Memorize all six — you'll meet them again on the Module 0 quiz, and for the rest of your life after that.
AbbreviationFull Latin FormTranslation
etc.et cetera“and the rest”
i.e.id est“that is”
e.g.exempli gratia“for the sake of an example”
A.M.ante meridiem“before midday”
P.M.post meridiem“after midday”
vs.versus“against”
Say each one aloud as abbreviation → full Latin → meaning: “etc. → et cetera → and the rest… i.e. → id est → that is…” and on through all six. Then say them in reverse order.
📚 PART 2 · DERIVATIVE MINUTE

et cetera → etc. Et means “and” (it appears unchanged in modern French as et), and cetera means “the remaining things.” Every time you write etc. in an essay, you are writing Latin.

versus → vs. This Latin preposition means “against” or “turned toward.” It entered English legal language directly — we still say “Smith versus Jones” in a lawsuit. It connects to the Latin verb vertere, “to turn,” which also gives us convert, divert, and reverse.
