📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What does it mean to study a language no one speaks natively?
No community anywhere on Earth speaks Latin as a first language today. That technically makes it a “dead” language. But its reach — through science, law, government, and the words in your own sentences — is still very much alive. Welcome to Latin. Today: why anyone still learns it, and where it came from.

📚 PART 1 · WHY ANYONE STILL LEARNS LATIN

You are about to learn a language that hasn't been anyone's first language in centuries — and that is exactly why it matters. For roughly 1,500 years after the fall of the western Roman Empire, Latin remained the shared language of scholars, lawyers, scientists, and religious thinkers all across Europe.

When the Roman senator Cicero gave speeches in 63 BC, he spoke in Latin. When the scientist Isaac Newton published his famous laws of motion in 1687 — over a thousand years after Rome had fallen — he still wrote in Latin. Learning Latin means learning to read that long written record for yourself.

📚 PART 2 · WHERE LATIN CAME FROM

From one small city to an empire spanning three continents

Latin began in Latium, a region in central Italy along the Tiber River. The small city of Rome, according to Roman tradition founded in 753 BC, sat at the center of it. As Rome grew — first into a republic, then into an empire — Latin spread with it.

Latium
a small region, one city: Rome  🏙️
By the 1st century AD
Britain to Mesopotamia (modern Iraq)  🌐

Everywhere Rome ruled, Latin followed: inscribed on monuments, written into legal documents, spoken in markets, used to run the government.

💡 Good to know: Rome was bilingual at its core. Greek stayed the dominant language of the eastern empire, and even educated Romans read Greek literature. When you study Latin, you're stepping into a much bigger Mediterranean conversation.
