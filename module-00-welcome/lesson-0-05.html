📚 PART 1 · HOW BIG WAS ROME?

At its height, around 117 AD under Emperor Trajan, the Roman Empire covered roughly 5 million square kilometers and perhaps 70 million people — somewhere between a fifth and a quarter of everyone alive on Earth at the time. It stretched from Scotland's Hadrian's Wall to the Persian Gulf.

West
Hispania, Gallia, Britannia, Italia — Latin was the language of daily life
East
Graecia, Asia Minor, Syria, Egypt — Greek stayed the dominant language

Latin isn't just the language of soldiers and bureaucrats, though it was that too. It is the language in which Roman law was written down, in which the poet Virgil composed the Aeneid, in which Cicero crafted some of the most influential prose in Western history, and in which Julius Caesar recorded his military campaigns in a style simple enough that schoolchildren have read it for two thousand years. That's a living inheritance you're starting to access.

📚 PART 2 · YOUR FIRST LATIN SENTENCE

You won't dig into Latin grammar in detail until Module 1. But you can already read one famous Latin phrase, in full:

Senatus Populusque Romanus
“The Senate and the Roman People” — abbreviated SPQR

This phrase was stamped on Roman coins, carved into monuments, painted on military standards, and printed on government documents throughout the Republic and Empire. You can still see SPQR on manhole covers in Rome today — the city government still uses it as its official emblem.

Notice one detail: Populusque contains the suffix -que, a Latin word meaning “and” that attaches to the end of a word instead of standing on its own before it. Latin often works differently from English in ways that will ask you to adjust your assumptions — that adjustment is exactly what this course trains.

📚 PART 3 · REVIEW BEFORE THE QUIZ

Before you move on, check that these five ideas from this week feel solid:

▶
“Dead language” means no living community speaks it natively — it does not mean the language is unimportant.
▶
French, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, and Romanian are “Romance languages” because they all evolved from spoken Latin.
▶
Roughly 60 percent of English vocabulary traces to Latin, mostly through the Norman Conquest and Renaissance borrowing.
