📚 PART 1 · A VERY OLD IDEA

Roman schoolchildren wrote daily work on wax tablets with a stilus, a pointed stylus — the flat end could smooth the wax for reuse. For anything meant to last, Romans used papyrus or parchment. Over time, the codex — a bound book of pages, the direct ancestor of the book you hold today — replaced the scroll.

Wax tablet (daily work)
→
Papyrus / parchment (permanent)
→
Codex (bound book, by the 4th century AD)

Every serious student of Latin from the Renaissance forward kept what scholars called a commonplace book (locus communis, literally “common place”) — a place to collect quotations, vocabulary, and grammar notes in one spot. The Renaissance scholar Erasmus organized his entire intellectual life around books like this. Your Latin notebook belongs to that same tradition, in a modern form.

📚 PART 2 · FOUR SECTIONS, ONE NOTEBOOK

Your notebook will hold four kinds of entries, built up week by week:

Paradigm Tables
organized charts of the Latin forms you're learning
Vocabulary Lists
Latin words with their meanings and English derivatives
Grammar Notes
your own explanations, in your own words, of how the Latin system works
Derivative Logs
English words you trace back to their Latin origins
