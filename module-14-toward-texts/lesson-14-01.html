What does it feel like to read real Latin, even with help?
Every sentence you've read so far this year was written just for you, a beginning Latin student. This week, that changes. You're going to read sentences adapted from things real Romans actually wrote — a general, a lawyer, and even a few gravestones. 📚 PART 2 · MEETING CAESAR

A general who was also a writer

Julius Caesar (about 100–44 B.C.) was a Roman general and politician whose career reshaped Rome forever. But he was also one of the clearest writers of Latin prose who ever lived. While he commanded Rome's armies in Gaul (roughly modern France) from 58–50 B.C., he wrote a year-by-year account of the campaign called the Bellum Gallicum (“the Gallic War”) and sent it home so Romans could follow his victories. He wrote about himself in the third person — “Caesar did this,” not “I did this” — which makes the writing sound calm and official, almost like a report.

Caesar's sentences are famous for being direct and his vocabulary manageable. For two thousand years, students beginning Latin have used Caesar as their first real Roman author. This week, you join them.

📚 PART 3 · WHAT “ADAPTED” MEANS

Adapted means a teacher has taken Caesar's real Latin and made small changes so a first-year student can read it: simplifying a hard sentence, swapping in a more common word, and glossing (giving you the meaning of) any vocabulary or grammar you haven't studied yet. The vocabulary is adjusted. But the grammar you already know — the endings, the cases, the verbs — is completely real.

💡 Word order is freer in Latin.
In English, word order carries the meaning: “The soldier conquers the enemy” means something completely different from “The enemy conquers the soldier.” In Latin, the endings carry the meaning, so word order can move around. Miles hostem vincit and Hostem miles vincit mean the exact same thing — “the soldier” is nominative (the subject) wherever it sits, and “the enemy” is accusative (the object) wherever it sits. Your job as a reader is to read the endings, not the order.
