📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How do you read a real Latin passage without knowing every word?
Today you read six real adapted sentences from Caesar's Bellum Gallicum — the actual document he sent home from Gaul. Every word you haven't studied is glossed for you. Your job is to use the reading toolkit you already own: nominative, accusative, and esse.

📚 PART 1 · A READING STRATEGY

Real Latin feels different from a practice sentence built just for you. Here is the strategy Roman schoolchildren's teachers still recommend today:

1. Read it once, all the way through.
Do not stop at the first unfamiliar word. Get the shape of the whole passage first.
2. Read it again, sentence by sentence.
Find the verb. Find its subject (nominative). Find its object, if any (accusative).
3. Use the gloss, not a guess.
Any word you have not studied will be given to you underneath the passage. Look it up instead of guessing from English.
📚 PART 2 · THE ADAPTED PASSAGE

This passage is adapted from Caesar's Bellum Gallicum (Book I, chapters 1–2).

Caesar in Gallia erat. Gallī agricolae erant. Rōmānī milites in terra Gallorum erant. Caesar imperator magnus erat. Milites Rōmānī terram Gallicam amabant. Senatus Rōmānus Caesarem laudabat.
Vocabulary Gloss
Gallia, -ae (f.) — Gaul (the region)  ·  Gallī, -ōrum (m. pl.) — the Gauls (the people)  ·  agricola, -ae (m.) — farmer  ·  miles, militis (m.) — soldier (given — 3rd declension, studied in S2)  ·  terra, -ae (f.) — land  ·  imperator, -ōris (m.) — general, commander (given)  ·  magnus, -a, -um — great, large (given — adjective, studied in S2)  ·  laudābat — was praising (given — imperfect tense, S2; treat as “was ___ing”)  ·  amābant — were loving/liked (given — imperfect plural)  ·  senatus, -ūs (m.) — senate (given)
Forms you already own: erat, erant (esse, Module 10). Caesarem (accusative — object). Rōmānī (nominative plural, 2nd decl. masc., Module 7). terram (accusative singular, 1st decl., Module 4). Gallōrum is genitive plural — a “whose?” ending you have seen in cultural notes but not formally studied; here it simply means “of the Gauls.”
