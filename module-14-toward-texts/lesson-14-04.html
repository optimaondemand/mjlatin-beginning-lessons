What happens when the greatest Latin prose stylist ever known writes a simple sentence?
You meet a second real Roman author today: Cicero, a lawyer and politician whose Latin generations of students imitated. You'll run a step-by-step reader's checklist on his adapted passage, then look at what ordinary Romans carved into stone.
🎵 MEMORY WORK · PARSE BEFORE YOU READ
Before you read today's lesson, parse this short Roman gravestone inscription in your notebook — work from memory, do not look anything up:
Marcia puella bona hic est. (“Marcia, a good girl, is here.”)
Write: (1) the case and function of Marcia; (2) the case and function of puella; (3) what est tells you about person, number, and tense. (bona is an adjective — take as given; hic means “here” and is not a noun form.)

📚 PART 1 · MEETING CICERO

Marcus Tullius Cicero (106–43 B.C.) is, by most measures, the greatest Latin prose stylist who ever lived. He was not a general like Caesar — he was a lawyer and politician who rose to Rome's highest office, consul, almost entirely through the force of his speaking and writing. His most famous political act as consul was suppressing a conspiracy led by Catiline to overthrow the government; he defended that decision, and its consequences, for the rest of his life.

Cicero's real Latin is long and complex, full of subordinate clauses and careful rhythm. The passage below is simplified considerably — but even simplified, you are reading a man who spent his whole adult life crafting words with conscious artistry. Every word choice was deliberate.

📚 PART 2 · THE READER'S CHECKLIST

Run this checklist on every sentence: (1) find the verb, (2) find its nominative subject, (3) find its accusative object if there is one, (4) check the gloss for anything unfamiliar, (5) translate.

Rōma magna est. Rōmānī cives boni sunt. Senatus Rōmānus in foro est. Oratores in foro orant. Cicero orator clarus erat. Cicero patriam amabat. Patria in periculo erat.
Vocabulary Gloss
cives (m. pl., nom.) — citizens (given — 3rd decl., studied in S2)  ·  forum, -ī (n.) — the Forum, Rome's public square  ·  orator, -ōris (m.) — a speaker (given)  ·  orant — they speak/plead (1st conj., 3rd pl. present — you know this form)  ·  clarus, -a, -um — famous, distinguished (given — adjective)  ·  patria, -ae (f.) — fatherland, homeland  ·  periculum, -ī (n.) — danger  ·  amābat — was loving/loved (given — imperfect)
Forms you already own: est, sunt, erat (esse). patriam (accusative singular, 1st decl., Module 4). The phrases in forō and in periculō use the same “in + noun” reading chunk from yesterday — treat each as a glossed unit meaning “in the Forum” and “in danger.”
📚 PART 3 · WHAT STONE RECORDS

Not everything Romans wrote was a book. Most Romans — including the many who were not wealthy or highly educated — encountered Latin on stone: gravestones, public buildings, military equipment. A typical Roman gravestone followed a pattern: the abbreviation D.M. (Dis Manibus, “to the divine shades”), then the name, then vixit annos (“lived ___ years”). It was as standardized as “R.I.P.” today. Stone survives because it is harder than time — the same grammar you are learning carried a general's propaganda and an ordinary family's grief alike.
