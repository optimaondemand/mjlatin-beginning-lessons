📚 PART 1 · WHY ROMANS WROTE

In Rome, gloria (glory, fame) and the approval of the senatus (Senate) were essential to a politician's power. Caesar was fighting on the edge of the known world while rivals schemed against him back in Rome. The Bellum Gallicum was a weapon in that political battle: a published document that put his version of events into every educated Roman's hands. When you read Senatus Rōmānus Caesarem laudābat (“The Roman Senate was praising Caesar”), you are reading propaganda as well as Latin prose — the words were chosen carefully.

This is what it means to read an authentic text: you are not just practicing grammar. You are reading something a real person wrote under real pressure, for a real purpose, roughly 2,070 years ago. It has survived because Romans copied it, medieval monks preserved it, and Renaissance scholars printed it. You are one more link in that chain.

📚 PART 2 · DERIVATIVE MINUTE

Flip each card to see the English word this Latin word left behind.

gloria
tap to flip
glory, glorious. The honor earned through military or civic achievement — think “Old Glory,” the American flag.
victoria
tap to flip
victory, victorious. From vincere, “to conquer.” The Roman goddess Victoria was shown with wings, a wreath, and a palm branch.
senatus
tap to flip
senate, senator. From senex, “old man” — the Senate was literally a council of elders.
imperator
tap to flip
emperor, imperial. From imperare, “to command.” After Caesar, the word shifted from a battlefield title to the permanent title of Rome's ruler.
terra
tap to flip
terrain, territory, terrestrial, Mediterranean. Mare + terra = “the sea in the middle of the land.”
