📚 PART 1 · THE WEEK ON ONE PAGE
Adapted means the vocabulary is simplified and glossed for you — the grammar is completely real.
Word order is free in Latin. Read the endings, not the position.
The reader's checklist: (1) find the verb · (2) find its nominative subject · (3) find its accusative object, if any · (4) check the gloss · (5) translate.
You met two real Roman authors this week — Caesar, the general who wrote to build gloria and win the Senate's favor, and Cicero, the lawyer whose careful prose generations of students imitated. You also read how ordinary Romans recorded names and lives in stone.
📚 PART 2 · ONE MORE INSCRIPTION

Run the checklist on this pair of short adapted inscriptions:

Miles Rōmānus hic iacet.
Senatus Populusque Rōmānus victoriam celebrat.
iacet — lies (buried) (given)  ·  hic — here (adverb, not declined)  ·  Populusque — “and the people” (given)  ·  victoriam — accusative singular of victoria, direct object  ·  celebrat — celebrates, honors (1st conj., 3rd sg. present — you know this ending)
Read it out: “A Roman soldier lies here.” (a gravestone) — “The Senate and People of Rome celebrate the victory.” (a public monument). The second sentence is the famous formula SPQR (Senatus Populusque Rōmānus) — stamped on Roman buildings, military standards, and coins throughout the empire.
