📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How did the Romans talk about their world?
Roman geography gives you excellent parsing practice, because the sentences are short and declarative — a place, a fact, a claim. Today you parse full sentences about the Roman Mediterranean, and see how two of your Latin words became English ones.
📚 PART 1 · MARE NOSTRUM

The Romans called the Mediterranean Sea Mare Nostrum — “Our Sea.” By the height of the empire, Rome controlled nearly the entire coastline around it: North Africa, Spain, Gaul, Italy, Greece, and the eastern provinces. To a Roman, the Mediterranean was not a boundary between peoples — it was a Roman lake, a highway connecting the provinces.

Sailors — nautae — were essential figures in this world, carrying goods and people between provinces. When Roman writers described geography, their sentences were built almost entirely from the words you already know: places, esse, amat, and a short list of particles and prepositions.

Rōma non est provincia; Italia est provincia magna. (magna = great, given)
Parse it: Rōma = 1st decl., nom. sg., subject. Italia = 1st decl., nom. sg., subject. Provincia (both times) = 1st decl., nom. sg., predicate nominative.
“Rome is not a province; Italy is a great province.” Two nominatives, two different jobs — both linked by esse.

nauta

nautical, astronaut — astro (star) + nauta (sailor) = “star sailor.” Even cosmonaut uses the Greek equivalent of the same idea.
via
tap to reveal
via, viaduct, trivial — a trivium was where three roads (tri + via) met: a public crossroads where gossip circulated. That's why unimportant chatter became “trivial.”
