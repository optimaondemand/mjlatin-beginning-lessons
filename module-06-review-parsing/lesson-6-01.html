📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Can you identify what every word in a Latin sentence is doing?
This week has no new grammar. You already know every ending you need — nominative and accusative, singular and plural, for the 1st declension. What's new is the habit: naming, out loud, exactly what each word is doing before you translate. Roman students called this parsing, and they did it with every sentence they read.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to state the parsing notation format: declension, case, number.
2
I will be able to parse any 1st-declension noun I already know using that format.
3
I will be able to connect the English word “parse” to the Latin it came from.

📚 PART 1 · WORDS WE KNOW

The very word you're about to learn — parsing — is itself a Latin fingerprint on English. Warm up with two words that grew from the Latin you're using this module.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
The Latin word pars means “part.” Which English word do you think grew from it?

A second word worth knowing: the Latin casus means “a fall.” Roman grammarians pictured a noun's basic form standing upright, and every other form as a “fall” away from it. That word gives us the grammatical term case — and also the English word casual (something that happens “by chance,” the way a die falls).

📚 PART 2 · THE PARSING FORMULA

Three things, in this order: declension · case · number

You've been noticing endings since Module 1. Now you'll state what you notice in one fixed format, every time:

puellam = 1st decl., acc. sg.

Every noun you know so far is 1st declension, so that part never changes this semester. What you're really identifying is the case (nominative or accusative) and the number (singular or plural). Here's the full paradigm you already own:

SingularPlural
Nominativepuellapuellae
Accusativepuellampuellās
💡 Say it before you write it: read the ending first, name the case and number second, write the notation third. -am → accusative, singular → 1st decl., acc. sg.
