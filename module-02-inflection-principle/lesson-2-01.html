📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What does a word's ending tell you that word order cannot?
Read this: “The dog bit the man.” Now read this: “The man bit the dog.” Same words, completely different meaning — because English decides who-does-what by word order. Latin decides it a different way: by changing the ending of the word. That single fact is the key to this entire course.

2

3

📚 PART 2 · THE ENDING DOES THE WORK

English uses word order. Latin uses endings.

In English, “The dog bit the man” and “The man bit the dog” mean opposite things, because English tells you who's who by where the word sits. Move the words, and the meaning flips.

Latin does not depend on position this way. Latin marks a noun's job — subject, object, or something else — by changing its ending. This is called inflection, and a language built this way is an inflected language. Latin is one of the most thoroughly inflected languages of the ancient world.

💡 Here's why this matters for the whole year: because Latin's endings carry the meaning, a Roman writer could arrange words in almost any order for style or emphasis without losing clarity. This is why Latin has declensions (noun-ending patterns) and conjugations (verb-ending patterns) — you'll meet both this year.

Here's the surprising part: you already do this in English — just with pronouns, not every noun.

He laughed.
subject form — he is doing the laughing
The crowd watched him.
object form — he is being watched

“Him laughed” sounds wrong to your ear immediately — because English pronouns are still inflected. Latin simply applies this same idea to every noun, not just pronouns. In grammar, these different forms are called cases: he is the nominative case (subject form), and him is the accusative case (object form). This module introduces only the nominative — you'll meet the accusative later this year.
