📚 PART 2 · A WORLD RULED IN LATIN

By the second century AD, the Roman Empire stretched from Britannia in the northwest to Africa in the south, from Hispania in the west to lands far in the east. A provincia was a territory under Roman rule, governed by a Roman magistrate sent out from Rome after his year in office — the word originally meant the sphere of duty assigned to that official, and came to mean the territory where the duty was carried out.

Gallia was conquered by Julius Caesar in a brutal eight-year campaign (58–50 BC), which he documented himself in a text so clear that Latin students have read it for two thousand years. Britannia was invaded by Caesar twice (55 and 54 BC) and became a permanent province under the Emperor Claudius in 43 AD. Africa as a province meant the region around Carthage — land Rome seized after destroying the city in 146 BC, at the end of the Third Punic War.

💡 When you write Britannia provincia est — “Britain is a province” — you are using the exact terminology Roman administrators and writers used to describe their own empire.
📚 PART 3 · DERIVATIVE MINUTE

These English words descend straight from today's vocabulary:

insula → insular, insulate
provincia → province, provincial
Africa → African
Italia → Italian, italic
Roma → Roman, Romance

“Provincial” can mean narrow or unsophisticated — someone whose world doesn't extend past their own province. The Romance languages (French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Romanian) are literally the “languages of Rome.”
