📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How can two nouns in the same sentence take the same case?
In “Caesar is a general,” Caesar and “a general” are the same person — nothing is being done to anyone. Latin marks this special relationship by putting both nouns in the nominative case. Today you build your first real Latin sentences using this pattern.

📚 PART 1 · WHEN TWO NOUNS ARE EQUATED

In Lesson 2.01 you learned that the nominative case marks the subject — the noun doing the action. Now add one refinement: the nominative is also used for the predicate nominative — a noun that follows a linking verb (a verb of being, not action) and renames or describes the subject.

Caesar conquered Gaul.
Gaul is the direct object — acted upon. (In Latin: accusative.)
Caesar is a general.
“a general” is the predicate nominative — Caesar and the general are the same person. (In Latin: nominative.)
💡 Because both the subject and the predicate nominative take the nominative ending, a Latin reader knows instantly that esse is linking two equal nouns, not describing an action on an object. English relies on knowing “is” is a linking verb; Latin builds the equivalence right into the endings.
📚 PART 2 · BUILD YOUR FIRST REAL LATIN SENTENCES

The pattern is simple: subject (nominative) + predicate nominative + est. These are genuine Latin sentences — not simplified examples invented for learners. Romans such as the geographer Pomponius Mela described their provinces in exactly this kind of sentence.

Italia provincia est. Italy is a province.
Gallia provincia est. Gaul is a province.
Sicilia insula est. Sicily is an island.

A checklist for finding the predicate nominative:

1
Find the verb. Is it a form of esse (a linking verb, not an action)?
2
If yes, the noun before est is usually the subject, and the noun right after it renames the subject — that's your predicate nominative.
3
Check: does the sentence mean “X is Y,” not “X does something to Y”? If so, you've confirmed it.
