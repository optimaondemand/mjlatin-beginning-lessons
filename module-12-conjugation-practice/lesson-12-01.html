📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Can you hold a noun and a verb in mind at the same time?
No new grammar this week — you already know every noun ending and every verb ending you need. This module asks you to do something harder: read a full Latin sentence and track the noun and the verb at once, so you can tell whether they actually agree. That skill is called parsing, and trained Latin readers have used it for two thousand years.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what it means to parse a word — stating every grammatical fact its ending shows.
2
I will be able to state both parsing formulas from memory: nouns (case — number — declension — function) and verbs (person — number — tense — conjugation).
3
I will be able to identify the subject, verb, and object in a simple Latin sentence.

📚 PART 2 · TWO FORMULAS, ONE SENTENCE

What “parsing” actually means

To parse a Latin word is to state, in order, everything its ending shows. This is not the same as translating — translating gives you the English sentence; parsing is the analytical work that makes an accurate translation possible. You already know both formulas separately. Now you use them together.

FOR A NOUN
Case — Number — Declension — Function
FOR A VERB
Person — Number — Tense — Conjugation

Watch both formulas work on one real sentence about Julius Caesar's campaigns in Gaul:

Caesar Galliam superat. — Caesar overcomes Gaul.
WordParsing
Caesarnominative, singular, subject (a proper name)
Galliamaccusative, singular, 1st decl., direct object
superat3rd person, singular, present, 1st conj.
💡 Notice: Caesar is singular and superat ends in -at — 3rd singular. They agree. That check — does the verb's person and number match the subject? — is the whole game this module.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Every Latin word answers a question. A noun answers: case, number, declension, function. A verb answers: person, number, tense, conjugation. Hold both at once, and you can parse anything.
