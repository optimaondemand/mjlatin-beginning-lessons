📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How do you catch a sentence that's almost right?
A subject-verb agreement mistake in Latin is not a small thing — because endings carry so much information, a mismatch is a real structural crack, not just an awkward phrase. Today you drill the full verb chant one more time, then practice catching agreement errors on purpose.

📚 PART 1 · YOUR EAR ALREADY KNOWS
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
Would you ever say in English, “They loves the beach”? What does your ear tell you, and why?
📚 PART 2 · THE AGREEMENT CHECKLIST

Every time you meet a Latin sentence, run this check before you trust your translation:

1
Find the subject (the nominative noun). Is it singular or plural?
2
Check the verb ending. Singular endings: -ō, -ās, -at. Plural endings: -āmus, -ātis, -ant.
3
Do they match? A plural subject demands a plural verb. A singular subject demands a singular verb. No exceptions.
💡 Watch for compound subjects: when et joins two subjects, together they are plural — even if each one alone is singular. Nauta et agricola laborant. (The sailor and the farmer work.)

Now practice: does each sentence pass the checklist, or does it fail?

✅ SPOT THE ERROR
Is the sentence correct, or does it have an agreement error?
Nauta navigant. (a sailor... they sail?)
Puellae parant. (The girls prepare.)
Agricolae terram parat. (the farmers... prepares the land?)
Servī dōna portant. (The slaves carry the gifts.)

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A plural subject always demands a plural verb; a singular subject always demands a singular verb. Say it before you close the page: check the ending, not just the meaning.
