📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What did Romans watch, prepare for, and give?
Seven new 1st-conjugation verbs join your list this week. Every one of them describes something Romans actually did — crowding into the Circus Maximus, marching to war, or handing a gift to a friend who owed them a favor.
🎵 MEMORY WORK · NEW VOCABULARY
Add these seven verbs to your notebook. Each takes the same six-form paradigm you already know:
spectō, spectāre — to watch, observe
errō, errāre — to wander; to be mistaken
properō, properāre — to hurry
parō, parāre — to prepare
putō, putāre — to think, consider
vocō, vocāre — to call, summon
dō, dāre — to give (a little irregular — more on this in Lesson 11.04)

📚 PART 2 · SPECTATORS, ARMIES, AND PATRONS

Romans were enthusiastic spectators

The public games (ludī), chariot races at the Circus Maximus, and gladiatorial combat all drew enormous crowds. Roman writers describe these scenes with exactly the plural verbs you now know: the citizens watch (spectant) together.

Roman armies did not simply march into battle — they engaged in careful preparation: gathering supplies, training troops, scouting terrain. When the historian Livy describes armies getting ready for a campaign, he builds sentences shaped exactly like the ones you're learning to write.

🏆 Gift-giving as social contract:
Roman society ran on patronage — a network where a powerful patron gave (dat) gifts, protection, and support, and in return received loyalty and public deference. Augustus's own official record of his achievements is largely a catalogue of what he gave to Rome. When you see dō in a Roman text, this is usually the world behind it.

One more word carries a double meaning worth knowing: errō means both to physically wander and to be mistaken. Cicero uses it often when writing about how unexamined opinions lead the mind astray — the same word for a wandering foot and a wandering mind.
