📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why does the Roman world sort itself into three grammatical types?
Lands, people, and things — the Roman world Julius Caesar wrote about is built entirely from the three noun types you know. Today you'll place that world on a map, meet two important proper nouns, and trace today's words into English.
📚 PART 1 · LANDS, PEOPLE, THINGS

To Roman eyes, the world sorted itself neatly by grammar:

1st decl. fem. — lands
Gallia (Gaul), Britannia (Britain), Italia (Italy), Sicilia (Sicily), Germania (the Germanic lands)
2nd decl. masc. — people
populus (the people), dominus (master), servus (slave), amicus (friend)
2nd decl. neut. — things
oppidum (fortified town), bellum (war), regnum (kingdom), forum (public square), templum (temple)

Notice: geographical names lean strongly feminine (1st declension) — because terra, “land,” is itself feminine. Reading Caesar's world through Roman grammar is itself a piece of Roman culture.

📚 PART 3 · THE SENATE, CAESAR, AND THE RES PUBLICA

Senatus (the Senate) was the governing council of the Roman Republic, an assembly of elite men whose decrees carried enormous political weight. Caesar — Gaius Julius Caesar, a Roman general and author who lived roughly 100–44 BCE — conquered Gaul, then crossed the Rubicon River in 49 BCE in defiance of the Senate's order to disband his army. That crossing is why “crossing the Rubicon” still means taking an irreversible, decisive action. Both Senatus and Caesar appear in this module as fixed proper nouns — not vocabulary you decline through a paradigm, but names you'll meet again when you read Caesar's own Latin.

One more term worth knowing: res publica, “the public thing,” was the Romans' own name for their system of government — and it is the direct source of our English word republic.

📚 DERIVATIVE MINUTE
senatus → senate, senator, senior
Caesar → czar/tsar, Kaiser
populus → popular, republic
forum → forum, forensic
regnum → regal, reign
