📚 PART 2 · ROMAN PUBLIC SPACE AND POWER

The forum was far more than a marketplace. In every Roman town it was the civic center — the place where courts met, speeches were given, and citizens encountered the official life of the state. Rome's own Forum Romanum was so central to public life that when Julius Caesar was assassinated in 44 BC, his body was carried there, and Mark Antony delivered his famous funeral speech on that very spot.

The oppidum — a fortified town — was the basic unit of how Rome organized new territory. As Rome expanded into Gaul (modern France), it met existing Celtic hilltop oppida. Julius Caesar's own war memoir, Commentarii de Bello Gallico (Commentaries on the Gallic War), uses the word oppidum hundreds of times.

Imperium is the hardest of today's words to translate in one line. At its root, it meant the legal authority to command an army — granted by the Roman people to elected officials for a set time. When Julius Caesar led his army across the Rubicon River in 49 BC without authorization, he broke that rule and started a civil war. Over time, imperium grew to also mean the whole territory a Roman commander's authority reached — which is exactly how we get the English word empire.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Ten neuter nouns — bellum, templum, oppidum, regnum, forum, verbum, donum, caelum, periculum, imperium — aren't just vocabulary. They're the words Romans used to name war, worship, towns, kingdoms, courts, speech, gifts, the sky, danger, and command itself.
