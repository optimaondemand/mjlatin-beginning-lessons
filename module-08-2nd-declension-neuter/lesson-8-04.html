📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If two words could each be the subject, how do you decide?
From here on, parsing a noun means more than naming its case and number — it means naming its declension too, and, for neuter nouns, reasoning out the case from the sentence itself.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name a noun's declension, case, and number together from its ending.
2
I will be able to use logic — not endings alone — to decide the case of an ambiguous neuter noun in a sentence.
3
I will be able to apply this reasoning to new sentences built only from esse and amat.
📚 PART 1 · A SENTENCE WITH TWO POSSIBLE SUBJECTS

Read this sentence: Forum verbum amat. (amat = loves, values). Both forum and verbum are neuter — so by the neuter rule, either one could be the subject or the object just from its ending. You have to reason it out.

The forum was Rome's place of courts and public speech — the whole point of the forum was words: arguments, laws, speeches. A word does not “value” a forum in that same civic sense; a forum, as the stage for oratory, plausibly does value words. So: forum is nominative (the subject, the thing doing the valuing) and verbum is accusative (the object, the thing valued). Translation: “The forum values speech.”

💡 This is not guessing. It's reading with intelligence — the same move every fluent reader of Latin makes whenever a neuter noun's ending won't settle the question alone.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

When a neuter noun's ending won't settle the case, ask: which noun can plausibly do the action, and which one is plausibly on the receiving end? That question, not the ending, gives you the answer.
