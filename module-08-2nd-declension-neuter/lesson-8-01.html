📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What if a Latin noun is neither masculine nor feminine?
So far every noun you've met has been feminine (like puella) or masculine (like servus). Today you meet a third kind — the neuter — and one rule about it that you will use for the rest of the year.

I will be able to explain what the neuter gender is and where the Latin word comes from.
2
I will be able to state the neuter rule word for word.
3
I will be able to spot the dictionary abbreviation n. and find a neuter noun's stem.

📚 PART 2 · THE THIRD GENDER

Neither one nor the other

The Latin word neuter literally means “neither one nor the other.” A neuter noun is grammatically neither masculine nor feminine — it is its own category, and it behaves by its own rule.

Here is a neuter noun's dictionary entry: bellum, bellī n. The little n. tells you the gender instantly. The first form (bellum) is the nominative singular. The second (bellī) is the genitive singular — drop the -ī and you have the stem: bell-. Every other form is built on that stem, exactly the way you already find the stem of a 2nd-declension masculine noun like servus, servī m. (stem serv-).

bellum (nom.)
war — the subject
bellum (acc.)
war — the object
💡 The neuter rule (memorize this word for word): In all neuter nouns, across all declensions, nominative and accusative are always identical. That is not a typo above — bellum really is both the subject form and the object form.
