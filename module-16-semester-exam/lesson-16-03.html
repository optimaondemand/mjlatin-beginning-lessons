📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What did this semester's Latin words give to English — and to you?
No new grammar today. This lesson is a celebration: a chance to see how many Latin words you now carry around inside English words you already knew, and to look back at the Roman world those words came from. You've earned this look back.

📚 PART 3 · THE ROMAN WORLD YOU'VE WALKED THROUGH

This semester you didn't just learn endings — you stepped into a world. You met Roman towns ringed by gates and ditches, with granaries full of copiae (troops). You met Cicero, arguing cases in the law courts of Rome and declaring, in three tightly inflected words, Civis Rōmānus sum — “I am a Roman citizen.” You met the motto stamped over every act of the Roman state, Senātus Populusque Rōmānus, SPQR: the Senate and the Roman People. You met Julius Caesar, describing his own campaigns in plain, precise Latin sentences built from exactly the endings you now know.

And you started reading real, adapted Latin about how that world actually worked. Read this passage once more — the same one from Module 16's grammar review — and notice how easily it reads now:

Rōma magna et pulchra est. In Rōmā multī hominēs habitant. Agricolae in agrīs laborant. Nautae ad portum nāvigant. Servī aquam et cibum portant. Dominī in villīs magnīs habitant. Puellae et puerī in viīs spectant. Omnēs Rōmānī sunt.
Read it out: “Rome is large and beautiful. In Rome, many people live. The farmers work in the fields. The sailors sail to the harbor. The slaves carry water and food. The masters live in large villas. The girls and boys watch in the streets. All are Romans.”
