📚 PART 1 · THE SUBJECT LIVES INSIDE THE VERB

One ending, one subject, no extra word needed

In English, the subject is always a separate word standing in front of the verb: I love, you love, he loves. In Latin, the subject is built into the ending. You never need a separate word for “I” — the ending -ō already means it.

amō
I love  — a complete sentence by itself
amās
you love  — one person, addressed directly
amat
he/she/it loves
💡 This is why Latin word order is so flexible. Because the verb already announces who is acting, a separate subject noun is optional — Romans added one mainly for clarity or emphasis. When a subject noun does appear (like puella), it stands in the nominative case, and the verb must still agree with it.
📚 PART 2 · THE WHOLE PATTERN

Here is the full singular present tense in one grid. Just like a noun paradigm, one small ending answers a big question — who?

Endingamō
1st — I-ōamō
2nd — you-āsamās
3rd — he/she/it-atamat
💡 This is a paradigm — a pattern, just like the noun paradigms you already know. Learn it once for amō, and this same set of three endings works for every verb in this module: laudō, portō, habitō, navigō, superō, laborō… you'll meet them all tomorrow.
