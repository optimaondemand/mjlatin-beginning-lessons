📚 PART 1 · TWO NOUNS, TWO JOBS

A transitive verb like amō takes a direct object — the love travels from the subject to something else. Case endings, not position, tell you which noun is which:

agricola
nominative — the subject: who loves
silvam
accusative — the object: what is loved
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
Agricola silvam amat means “The farmer loves the forest.” If you swapped the two Latin nouns' positions — Silvam agricola amat — would the meaning change?
📚 PART 2 · WHY WORD ORDER BARELY MATTERS

In English, “The girl loves the sailor” and “The sailor loves the girl” mean opposite things — position carries the grammar. In Latin, all three of these mean exactly the same thing:

Puella nautam laudat.
Nautam puella laudat.
Laudat puella nautam.

All three say “The girl praises the sailor.” Puella is always the subject because it is nominative; nautam is always the object because it is accusative — no matter where each word sits. Latin does lean toward Subject–Object–Verb order by default, but a writer can rearrange words for emphasis without changing who did what to whom.

💡 Watch out: swap the ENDINGS, not just the order, and the meaning really does flip. Nauta puellam laudat means “The sailor praises the girl” — a different sentence, because now nauta (not puella) is nominative.
📚 PART 3 · THE READER'S CHECKLIST

When you read (or build) a Latin sentence, run this quick check:

1
Find the nominative noun. That's your subject — who's acting.
2
Find the accusative noun. That's your direct object — what's receiving the action.
3
Check the verb ending. This module, every noun subject is singular, so the verb should end in -t.
