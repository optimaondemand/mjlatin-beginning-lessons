📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How is conjugating a verb like declining a noun?
All year you've been changing the endings of nouns to show their job and their number: puella → puellam → puellae. Latin verbs work the very same way. A verb's ending doesn't just sit there — it tells you who is doing the action and how many of them there are. Today the Latin verb system opens up, starting with the verb amō — “I love.”

📚 PART 2 · FOUR PARTS, ONE VERB

A Latin verb's dictionary entry has four forms

Look up a noun in a Latin dictionary and you get two forms — puella, puellae. Look up a verb and you get four, called the principal parts. Here is the entry for “to love”:

PartFormMeaning
1stamōI love
2ndamāreto love
3rdamāvīI loved
4thamātum(in order) to love
💡 For now, only the first two matter. The 1st part (amō) is the “I” form you'll build everything from today. The 2nd part (amāre, the infinitive “to love”) hides the piece you need next: the stem.

Finding the stem

Remember how you found a noun's stem? You took puella, removed the ending, and got puell- — ready for any case ending. A verb's stem works the same way, but you start from the infinitive, not the “I” form:

amāre
the infinitive
→
amā—
remove -re
→
ama—
the working stem

That long ā shortens once endings attach, so ama- is the stem you'll actually build with. Just like puell- takes any noun ending, ama- takes any verb ending — tomorrow you'll attach the endings that make it say “I love,” “you love,” “he loves.”
