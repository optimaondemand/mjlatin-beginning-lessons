📚 PART 2 · A DAY IN ROMAN LIFE

These verbs are not random — they are Roman daily life

A Roman farmer (agricola) laborat (works) his fields every day. The great statesman Cincinnatus was famously called away from plowing to serve Rome as dictator in a crisis — and when the danger passed, he went back to farming. Romans told that story for centuries as the model of duty over ambition.

A Roman sailor (nauta) navigat (sails) routes across the whole Mediterranean, which Romans called Mare Nostrum — “Our Sea.” During the First Punic War (264–241 B.C.), Rome had almost no navy at all — then built one from scratch, and the new Roman fleet eventually superat (overcomes) the experienced Carthaginian fleet. Navigō and superō together tell that whole story.

Public praise mattered enormously in Rome. A senator who served well was laudātus (praised) before the whole Forum, and funeral speeches — laudationes funebres — were delivered for the entire community to hear. Cicero, Rome's greatest orator, built his career on knowing exactly when to laudāre.

📚 PART 3 · DERIVATIVE MINUTE

Each of today's verbs left a mark on English:

laudō → applaud, laudatory
portō → transport, portable
habitō → habitat, inhabit
navigō → navigate, navy
superō → superior, insuperable
laborō → laboratory, elaborate
